There is a strange gap in the pop culture landscape. This is an era of infinite content. Every franchise with a pulse gets a streaming spin-off. Star Wars has a dozen shows. Marvel has more television than anyone can reasonably watch. Even Harry Potter, a property that spent years resisting expansion, is now getting a television reboot. But James Bond, a franchise that has been running for over sixty years and has grossed billions, has never had a proper television series.

Not one. In sixty years.

The question is why. The answer is more interesting than most people realize.

For most of Bond's existence, the franchise has been controlled by one family. Albert Broccoli produced the first Bond film in 1962. His daughter Barbara and stepson Michael G. Wilson took over in the 1990s. For over six decades, they had one guiding principle: Bond belongs in theaters.

This was not a business decision. It was a philosophical one.

Barbara Broccoli said it plainly. A television show, she explained, is a commitment of ten to twenty hours. A Bond film is two hours. The scale is different. The experience is different. Bond is an event. You go to the cinema, you sit in the dark, you watch the gunbarrel sequence, you hear the theme. Making a Bond series would dilute that. It would turn an event into a habit.

So the Broccoli family said no. Repeatedly.

In 2022, Amazon bought MGM. With that purchase came the distribution rights to the Bond films. Amazon now had a financial stake in the franchise, but not creative control. That still belonged to Broccoli and Wilson.

Amazon did not waste time. According to reports from The Hollywood Reporter, the streaming giant pitched multiple Bond television projects. One was a spin-off about Miss Moneypenny, the MI6 secretary who has been in nearly every Bond film since 1962. Another was about Felix Leiter, Bond's CIA ally. There was even talk of a female-led 007 series.

Broccoli and Wilson rejected all of them. According to sources, Wilson said he and his team "just try to focus on making good pictures." Broccoli was even more direct. A Bond TV show, she said, was "not something we've ever wanted to do."

There was one Bond television project the Broccoli family did approve. In 2023, Amazon Prime Video released 007: Road to a Million, a reality game show where contestants travel the world completing Bond-themed challenges. Brian Cox played "The Controller," a villainous mastermind watching from a distance. The prize was one million pounds.

It was, in the grand scheme of Bond, a minor thing. A game show, not a drama. Not canon. Not Bond.

And it did not work. Critics called it dull. Audiences did not connect. The second season was reworked so heavily it was almost a different show. By 2026, Amazon had quietly shelved it. The one time Bond went to television, it was a game show, and it failed.

If you want to be technical, there has been a Bond television series. In 1991, an American animation studio produced James Bond Jr., a cartoon about Bond's nephew attending a military academy and fighting a terrorist organization called SCUM. It ran for 65 episodes. Ian Fleming's estate approved it. Eon Productions was involved.

It was terrible.

The show is remembered, if it is remembered at all, as a curiosity. A footnote. A thing that happened because someone in the 1990s thought children needed a Bond of their own. It has no connection to the films. It is not canon. It is not Bond.

So when people say there has never been a Bond TV series, they mean there has never been a serious one. A prestige drama. A streaming epic. The kind of show that would sit alongside The Crown or Slow Horses or whatever else is dominating the cultural conversation.

That has never happened.

In February 2025, everything changed. Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson signed a deal with Amazon MGM Studios. The terms were simple: Amazon would take creative control of the Bond franchise. The Broccoli family would step back. For the first time in sixty years, James Bond belonged to someone else.

The deal was reported to be worth around twenty million dollars. For a franchise that has grossed over seven billion, that number seems impossibly small. But the Broccoli family was not selling for money. They were stepping away. The creative fight had worn them down.

With Amazon in control, the question of a Bond TV series is no longer a question of whether. It is a question of when.

Amazon has not announced a Bond series yet. The priority, reportedly, is Bond 26, the next film. There is no script, no director, no Bond. The search continues.

But Amazon is a streaming company. It builds franchises through television. It has done it with Lord of the Rings, with The Boys, with Reacher. The temptation to do the same with Bond will be enormous. A Moneypenny series. A Felix Leiter series. A young Bond series. A female 007 series. All of these were pitched before. All of them were rejected. None of them are off the table now.

Whether this is good or bad depends on what you want from Bond. If you want Bond to remain an event, a thing that happens every few years and demands your full attention, then the Broccoli family was right to say no. If you want more Bond, in more forms, available on demand, then Amazon is about to give you everything you ever wanted.

The gunbarrel sequence has always signaled the start of something. Now it might signal the start of a season.
