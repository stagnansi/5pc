Capcom released a new IP this year. Not Resident Evil. Not Monster Hunter. Not Street Fighter. A game called Pragmata, which nobody knew anything about until recently, set on a lunar research station with a man and a robot child. It sold two million copies in sixteen days. It scored around 85 on Metacritic. And it is one of the most interesting things the studio has made in years, not because it is perfect, but because it is trying something new in a space where new things rarely get made at this budget.

I did not expect to care about this game.

The premise sounds like something I have seen before. A man and a child, traveling together, protecting each other, discovering something about themselves along the way. The Last of Us did this. God of War did this. Every game with a dad and a kid has done this since 2013. But Pragmata does not pretend to be those games. It knows what it is. It is a video game, in the most unapologetic sense of the word, and it loves being one.

You play as Hugh Williams, an astronaut sent to a corporate research colony called the Cradle. Something goes wrong. The base's AI, IDUS, has gone rogue. Robots attack. Hugh is stranded, alone, until he finds Diana, a child android who has been living on the base. She is curious about everything. She wants to know what Earth is like. She wants to know what sand feels like. She draws pictures of the two of them with crayons. She is not a daughter, technically. She is company property. But the game treats her like a person, and so does Hugh, and so do you by the end.

The combat is where the game becomes itself. You shoot robots, but bullets do nothing until Diana hacks them first. Hacking is a puzzle. A small maze appears on screen while robots are shooting at you, and you draw a line from point to point, connecting blue nodes to reach a green target. The longer the line, the more damage you deal. The more nodes you connect, the longer the enemy's armor stays down. While you are doing this, you are also dodging, strafing, and firing. It is multitasking in the most literal sense. It sounds stressful, and it is, but it is also satisfying in a way that few combat systems are. You are not just shooting. You are solving a problem while being shot at, and when it works, it feels like you earned it.

You can play the game entirely through hacking if you want. You can play it entirely through gunplay. Or you can mix the two, building your character however you like. The game does not punish you for choosing a path. It just gives you more tools and lets you figure it out. There is a hub area where you can upgrade weapons, unlock new hacks, change your loadout, talk to Diana, play bingo with a robot, look at a museum of human history that Diana is slowly learning about. It is a lot of systems, and none of them feel forced. They feel like a game that is happy to be a game.

The story is not complicated. The plot is thin, honestly. There is a rogue AI, there is a villain named Eight who betrays everyone, there is a material called dead filament that spreads through organic matter and kills people. Hugh gets infected. He hides it from Diana. He sends her to Earth in a shuttle. He dies. That is the normal ending. It is bleak, and it is supposed to be. The game is not about saving the world. It is about getting one person to safety, and what it costs to do that.

There is a true ending. You can unlock a mod that cures the infection, and if you equip it before the final boss, Hugh survives. He comes back to the shelter. Cabin, the AI who runs the place, says, "Welcome back. Travelling alone today?" It is a small line. It changes everything. It is also optional. Most players will never see it.

I think that is the right choice. The normal ending is the one that matters. Pragmata is not a game about a man who gets to live. It is a game about a man who gives someone else a chance to live, and that is enough. The true ending is a reward for people who want it, but it is not the point. The point is the beach. The point is Diana seeing the ocean for the first time. The point is Hugh, knowing he will not make it, telling her what sand feels like, and meaning it.

It is not the best game Capcom has ever made. It is not the best game of the year. It is a game that tried something new, and it worked, and that is worth paying attention to.
